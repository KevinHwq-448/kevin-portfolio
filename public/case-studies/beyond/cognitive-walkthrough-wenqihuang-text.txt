inspired by Plato’s Allegory of the Cave. The project reimagines the
cave as a familiar college campus, where everyday life appears safe,
open, and ordinary on the surface. Beneath this normal environment,
however, a hidden system quietly shapes movement, information,

without question.

Final Concept
 Final Concept
In the hidden setting of Beyond the Shadows, this system was first established in
1998 by Julian Mercer. He was a key figure in campus space planning and
information system integration, believing that a "truly ideal campus" should not be
built on freedom and chance, but on predictable order, manageable behavior, and
optimized collective stability. Initially, the system was merely an auxiliary campus
experiment, used to analyze pedestrian flow, adjust space utilization efficiency,
optimize notification delivery and student behavior patterns, hoping to reduce
conflict, chaos, and unnecessary risks. However, as the system learned, its
function gradually shifted from "assisting in management" to "shaping reality"—it
no longer merely observed the campus, but began to decide what should be seen,
what should be ignored, what behaviors would be naturally encouraged, and what
ideas would be subtly suppressed in the environment.
Hidden settings
Hidden settings
Hidden settings
Hidden settings
Its control over students is not direct, brutal, or overt, but rather an
extremely quiet, institutionalized, and environmental influence. Through
campus route design, information distribution, spatial atmosphere, prompts,
visual symbols, and ubiquitous interface logic, the system gradually trains
students to form certain "default behaviors." Some areas always seem
brighter, safer, and more worthwhile to linger in; some paths are always
easier to choose; some information is always easier to receive, while others
simply disappear into the noise. Over time, students believe they are making
free choices, when in reality they are merely following pre-prepared tracks.
Its most terrifying aspect is not the commands themselves, but that it
makes obedience appear as a comfortable, normal, even rational way of life.

 Final Concept
Final Concept
 Final Concept
As time passed, this system became deeply embedded in the campus culture. Upperclassmen, staff,
and even some who had initially sensed something amiss gradually learned to coexist with it. They
might not know the whole truth, but they knew that "not delving too deeply" would make life easier.
Thus, a silent understanding formed on campus: the secret wasn't unknown, but rather that no one was
willing to openly admit it. Like the prisoner in Plato's Allegory of the Cave who mistook the projection for
reality, the people here had long accepted the system-filtered reality as the world truly was.
Julian Mercer "disappeared" shortly after the system's initial deployment. Official records state that he
simply withdrew from the project and left the campus, leaving no further explanation. However, deeper
archives reveal contradictory traces: some records indicate he attempted to terminate the system,
while others suggest he ultimately permanently sealed his permissions, thought patterns, and even a
certain "judgment logic" into the system's core. Because of this, later generations have never been able
to determine whether this system was merely Mercer's legacy, or whether it had evolved over time into
an independent will no longer needing its founder.
Hidden settings
Hidden settings
Hidden settings
Hidden settings
The truly unsettling aspect of the story isn't the students' violent control, but rather the
subtle calibration of their sense of reality. The system never needs to force everyone to
obey; it only needs to continuously shape the environment, making the majority believe
that order is more important than truth, stability is safer than doubt, and silence is easier
to survive than awakening. Thus, when the protagonist begins to investigate the truth, he
faces not just a hidden system, but a reality structure already maintained, accepted, and
lived within by everyone. In the end, he discovers that he is not the first to realize all this,
nor is he an observer standing outside the system—he himself may have already been
recorded, categorized, and written into a file by the system.

— The Participant
The Participant is the student who begins to notice small anomalies within the campus environment
and chooses to investigate what others ignore. At first, these irregularities seem minor — a strange
symbol, an unusual message, a subtle interface, or a pattern that feels slightly out of place. However,
instead of dismissing them, the Participant becomes increasingly attentive to the hidden structure
behind everyday campus life. Their role in the narrative is to move from curiosity to suspicion, and
eventually from suspicion to awakening, as they begin to question the reality that has long been
accepted as normal.

— The Collective
 In contrast, The Collective represents the wider campus population, who continue to live
within the system without openly resisting it. They are not necessarily completely unaware
of its presence; rather, they have adapted to its order, routines, and invisible logic over

treat it as natural, stable, and even comforting.

 Beyond the human characters, the narrative is driven by The Hidden System, the invisible structure that
shapes movement, information, and perception across the campus. It does not rely on open force or

The primary audience for Beyond the Shadows is college students and
young adults who are familiar with campus life and interested in mystery,
puzzle-solving, exploration, and hidden narrative experiences. It also speaks
to audiences who are drawn to speculative stories about institutional
control, constructed realities, and utopian systems beneath ordinary
environments. The project uses this familiarity as part of its design
strategy, beginning with a public clue placed in an ordinary campus-like
setting so that the experience feels believable and easy to enter. The
audience is then guided through a digital discovery platform that rewards
curiosity with hidden records, system traces, and deeper narrative context.
By the final immersive reveal, the user is no longer just observing the story,
but actively positioned inside it. In this way, the narrative is integrated
with audience behavior by moving from recognition, to investigation, to
personal implication.

How can we make this story suitable for them to enter, understand, and
continue exploring?
To make this story suitable for its audience, the experience should be
designed so that it feels easy to enter, clear to understand, and rewarding
to continue. Since the main audience includes college students, mystery
and puzzle enthusiasts, and people interested in speculative systems and
controlled realities, the story works best when it begins in a setting that
already feels familiar. Using a campus-like environment, a public clue, or a
QR-based entry point helps lower the barrier to entry because the first
interaction feels believable and natural rather than overly fictional or
complicated. This makes the audience feel that they are discovering
something hidden inside an ordinary world, which is more engaging than
simply being told the premise from the start.
To help the audience understand the story, the narrative should unfold
gradually instead of delivering all of its ideas at once. Each platform should
reveal a different layer of meaning: the first introduces the anomaly, the
second expands the hidden system through records, traces, and evidence,
and the third provides the final reveal. This step-by-step structure is
important because the story is based on questioning reality, so the
audience should experience that shift for themselves. Rather than reading a
full explanation immediately, they begin by noticing something strange,
then interpreting clues, and finally realizing that the system is much larger
than it first appeared. This makes the concept easier to follow while also
keeping the audience emotionally involved.

To encourage continued exploration, every stage should give the audience a
reason to move forward. The first stage should create curiosity, the second
should deepen the mystery, and the final stage should create personal
implication. In other words, the user should not feel like they are just
collecting information, but that they are being drawn further into the
system with every step. For puzzle-oriented users, this comes from
uncovering hidden details and connecting fragments. For audiences
interested in speculative or utopian control themes, it comes from gradually
realizing that the campus is not simply hiding a secret, but quietly shaping
reality itself. By moving from familiarity, to discovery, to implication, the
story becomes something the audience can enter naturally, understand
progressively, and feel motivated to keep exploring.

 Platform 1
Platform 1
 Platform 1
On the surface, it looks like an ordinary campus event poster, featuring a
seemingly reasonable, familiar, and unsuspecting campus exploration
activity. However, its true function isn't to promote the event itself, but
rather to naturally draw users into a hidden narrative through elements
familiar to students, such as a campus map, public event language, and QR
codes. In other words, it utilizes the most common forms of communication
on campus, disguising the story within a believable everyday scenario,
giving the audience a sense of "this seems real" before they further engage
with the system.
The strength of your poster lies in its avoidance of immediately revealing
any "mysterious system" or "control" information. It uses a very normal tone,
using phrases like "Campus Discovery Week," "student-led activities,"
"walking routes," and "printed prompts"—all resembling promotional material
for a real school event. The advantage of this approach is that users don't
feel suddenly thrown into an abstract setting, but rather perceive it as a
real-world entry point. The QR code here becomes the narrative trigger; once
the user scans it, they transform from "someone looking at the poster" into
"someone entering the story."

Platform 2
Platform 2
Platform 2
Users first scan a QR code on a poster to enter a seemingly normal event
page—Campus Discovery Week. This stage is crucial because it continues
the "trusted entry point" of Platform 1, convincing the audience it's just a
regular campus event. Then, the page is interrupted by FORCE OVERRIDE,
and the previously normal information is overwritten by system traces. At
this point, the narrative begins to shift; users realize for the first time that
they haven't entered a simple event page, but rather an entry point
occupied by a hidden system.
The subsequent encrypted and shift-decrypted pages serve the function of
"participatory discovery." Users are not passively reading the story but must
actively adjust, observe, and decipher it, transforming them from observers
into investigators. This is a perfect opportunity to emphasize in your
presentation: this platform doesn't directly tell the audience the truth, but
rather uses interaction to push the story from the surface to deeper system
awareness. This perfectly aligns with the "from familiarity to skepticism"
logic of your entire project.
The subsequent terminal, log file, decrypted intel, and system alert pages
actually constitute a complete digital narrative path. The terminal makes
users feel like they're entering a restricted system layer; the log file makes
the worldview more concrete, indicating that the system has permeated
daily campus life; the red decrypted intel directly exposes the system's
ideology, letting the audience understand that this is not ordinary
surveillance, but a control structure operating in the name of "order,
optimization, and stability"; the final system alert advances the story from
"I'm watching a secret" to "the system is also responding to me," a crucial
step because it personalizes the narrative.

 Platform 3
Platform 3
 Platform 3
 Platform 3 functions as the AR narrative climax of Beyond the Shadows.
While the earlier platforms invite the audience into the mystery and allow
them to investigate the hidden system through clues and digital records,
this platform is where the story becomes direct, spatial, and personal. It
moves the audience from observing the system on the outside to
experiencing it from within.
Within the game, previously fragmented information is brought together
through exploration, environment, archives, and restricted spaces. This
gives the earlier clues a stronger narrative payoff, allowing the player to
understand that the campus is not simply hiding a secret, but has long
been shaped by a structure of observation, classification, and control. In
this way, the platform transforms the story from a mystery about hidden
knowledge into a realization about constructed reality.
Most importantly, the game changes the player’s role. Instead of
remaining a distant investigator, the player begins to feel personally
implicated in the system itself. By the final stage, the hidden archive no
longer appears to belong only to others — it begins to suggest that the
player may already be part of it. As a result, Platform 3 ends the narrative
not with clear resolution, but with awakening, reinforcing the project’s
connection to Plato’s Allegory of the Cave.

Usabity testing
Usabity testing
Usabity testing
To evaluate user experience, I conducted usability tests across three platforms,
including A/B testing, interview/post-test questions, and task-based testing,
focusing on clarity, navigation, and narrative progression. Users were asked to
experience the poster entry point, the digital exploration phase, and the final game
experience sequentially, observing where they hesitated, felt confused, and which
parts were most engaging. The main issues were whether the entry point was clear
enough, whether the digital cues were easy to understand, and whether the final
game environment effectively conveyed the story.
The tests showed that users responded well to the game's sense of mystery and
atmosphere, but some users needed clearer guidance when transitioning from one
stage to the next. Based on this feedback, I optimized the call to action on the
entry platform, adjusted the pacing and readability of the digital exploration
interface, and strengthened the visual and spatial cues in the game, making the
final revelation more coherent and impactful.
1. Entry poster / QR code
Question:
When you first saw the poster, did you understand what to do next?
I understood that I was supposed to scan the QR code, because that part
was clear. At first, I thought it was just a normal school event, which made it
feel believable. But after scanning, I realized something felt strange, and that
made me want to keep going.
What this suggests:
The entrance is clear, and the design of "normal first, then abnormal" is
effective.
2. Poster believability
Question:
Did the poster feel like something that could really appear on a campus?
Yes, it looked believable because it used a campus map, event details, and a
normal layout. It didn’t immediately look like fiction, which made the
transition into the hidden story more interesting.
What this suggests:
The poster is believable enough and suitable for the entrance of the story.
3. Digital discovery clarity
Question:
When the event page changed into the system interface, was it clear that the
story was shifting?
Yes, I could tell something was wrong when the page was overridden. That
part was effective because it changed suddenly, but not so much that I got
lost. It felt like I was entering a hidden layer rather than just opening another
webpage.
What this suggests:
Platform 2’s turning point works, and users can feel the narrative shift.
4. Puzzle/decoding
Question:
Was the encrypted message and decoding interaction understandable?
Sample user response:
I understood that I needed to decode something, but I needed a moment to
figure out how the shift worked. Once I understood it, it was interesting
because it made me feel involved instead of just reading information.
What this suggests:
The decryption mechanism is attractive, but could use a clearer hint.
5. Game experience
Question:
In the final game platform, did you feel like you were exploring a hidden
system rather than just looking at a scene?
Yes, the environment made it feel like I was inside the system. The dark
space, terminals, and archive-like elements made it feel more immersive. It
felt less like reading a story and more like moving through it.
What this suggests:
Platform 3’s immersion is established.
6.Final reveal
Question:
By the end of the experience, did you understand what the system was doing
and why it mattered?
Sample user response:
I understood that the system wasn’t just hiding in the background, but
shaping how people move and what they notice. The ending made me feel like
the player might also be part of the archive, which made the story stronger.
What this suggests:
The theme at the end is basically understandable, and the point of "the
players are in the system" is valid.

Al as-a-tool Update
Al as-a-tool Update
Al as-a-tool Update
AI was used in Project 3 as a support tool for refinement, iteration, and development across the final three platforms. It helped me
clarify narrative details, refine the wording of story components, and strengthen how the experience moved from the public entry
poster, to the digital discovery platform, and finally into the interactive game experience. AI was also useful for testing visual
directions, generating supporting concept imagery, and exploring how archival materials, hidden system interfaces, and
environmental storytelling could be represented more clearly and consistently.
However, AI was not used to replace the design process. The final platform structure, visual direction, narrative logic, and overall
pacing of the experience were shaped through my own decisions and project goals. Many AI-generated suggestions were too
broad, too literal, or not fully aligned with the project’s tone of calm control, institutional order, and gradual awakening, so they
needed to be carefully selected, edited, and adapted. In this way, AI functioned more as a brainstorming and iteration tool than as
the author of the final work.
The most important creative decisions remained human-led. The project’s interpretation of Plato’s Allegory of the Cave, the
refinement of the three-platform experience, and the emotional progression from entry, to discovery, to final implication were all
developed through my own design decisions. The sound design for the final game platform was also shaped separately through
intentional audio placement, atmosphere building, and containment effects, helping the ending feel procedural, immersive, and
narratively controlled. This balance ensured that AI supported productivity and experimentation while the final project still
reflected my own creative direction.

Reflection
Reflection
Reflection
During Project 3, the project began to feel much more complete and real to me because I was no longer only developing the
concept — I had to make the narrative work across the final platforms in a clear and believable way. In the earlier stage,
Beyond the Shadows was still mainly exciting as an idea, with its hidden campus system, allegorical meaning, and
speculative atmosphere. But in Project 3, I started to see where the real challenges were. Once I began refining the poster
entry, the digital discovery sequence, and the final interactive game experience, I realized that a strong concept alone was
not enough. The project needed to guide the audience clearly from one stage to the next while still keeping the mystery
and atmosphere intact.
One of the biggest challenges for me was narrative clarity. At first, I was focused mainly on the world-building and the
hidden system itself, but as I developed the final platforms, I realized that users would only stay engaged if the experience
was easy to enter, understandable to follow, and rewarding to continue. This made me think much more carefully about
transitions, pacing, and how each platform contributed a different layer of information. The poster could not just look
interesting — it had to feel believable as a public entry point. The digital platform could not simply contain story material —
it had to gradually shift the audience from curiosity into investigation. The final game also could not just be a visual space
— it had to function as the emotional and narrative climax of the project.
At the same time, I became much more aware of how atmosphere supports storytelling. I did not want the final platform to
feel like only a technical environment or a collection of visual assets, so I spent more time refining environmental cues,
archives, system interfaces, and sound design. In particular, the sound layer in the game helped me understand that
immersion comes from many small details working together: ambient server noise, machine activation, mechanical door
sounds, and containment effects all helped make the hidden system feel procedural, controlled, and physically present.
This process made me think about the project less as separate media pieces and more as one connected narrative system,
where visual design, interaction, and atmosphere all need to reinforce the same story.
